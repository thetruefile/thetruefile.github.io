The Girl Who Fell 10,000 Feet and Survived 11 Days in the Amazon

Case file: Juliane Koepcke, LANSA Flight 508, Peru, December 24, 1971

Watch the 60-second Short

The flight

On Christmas Eve 1971, 17-year-old Juliane Koepcke and her mother boarded LANSA Flight 508 in Lima, headed for Pucallpa. The plane was hours late and everyone wanted to get home. Her father, a zoologist, was waiting for them at a research station deep in the rainforest.

Partway through the flight the plane flew into a thunderstorm. Violent turbulence shook the cabin. Juliane saw a bright light on one of the engines. Her mother said calmly, "That is the end, it's all over." Those were the last words she heard from her.

The fall

The aircraft broke apart in the air, about two miles above the ground. Juliane was thrown out, still strapped to her row of seats, spinning toward the treetops. She lost consciousness before impact. The next day she woke up in the rainforest and thought: I survived an air crash.

She had a broken collarbone, deep cuts on her legs and a damaged knee ligament. She had lost her glasses and one sandal. She was alone.

Water leads to people

Juliane had spent a year and a half at her parents' research station, so she knew the forest was less hostile than most people think. She also remembered what her father taught her: follow the water. Streams lead to rivers, and rivers lead to people.

She found a small creek and walked in it, using her remaining shoe to test the ground ahead. Snakes hide among dry leaves, and she could not see well without her glasses. She ate a bag of sweets she found at the wreck. When they were gone she had nothing. She heard search planes overhead but they could not see her through the canopy.

On the fourth day she followed the sound of king vultures and came upon three dead passengers, still strapped in their seats. For a moment she feared one was her mother. The woman's toenails were painted. Her mother never wore nail polish.

The hut

By day ten she could barely stand. She drifted along the bank of a larger river and found a boat, then a path leading to a hut with a palm-leaf roof, an outboard motor and a can of gasoline. An arm wound was infested with maggots. She remembered her father treating a dog the same way, poured a little gasoline into the wound and pulled out about thirty maggots. She decided to stay the night.

The next morning she heard voices. Local fishermen found her at the hut. At first they thought she was a water spirit from local legend. She introduced herself in Spanish and told them what had happened. They treated her wounds, fed her and took her back to civilization the next day.

What happened next

Juliane was the only survivor of the 92 people on board, 91 of whom died. A search party later found her mother. Juliane learned that she had survived the crash too, but was badly hurt and could not move. She died several days later. Juliane became a biologist who specializes in bats, and she still runs the Panguana research station where she grew up.

Investigators later concluded the aircraft failed in the storm, and cited flying into hazardous weather as a cause.
